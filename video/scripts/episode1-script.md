### CASE 001 — THE OLDEST MURDER — 1:00–3:20
*(location card: Sima de los Huesos — "the Pit of Bones" — Atapuerca, Spain)*

> Nineteen eighty-four. Deep in the Atapuerca mountains of northern Spain,
> researchers find a vertical shaft — forty-three feet straight down — that
> opens into a cave. Inside: the bones of at least twenty-eight people.
> Thousands of years of an entire population, dumped at the bottom of a pit.
>
> That alone was strange enough. Why were so many bodies in one hole?
>
> Then, in 2015, scientists reassembled one skull from fifty-two fragments.
> They called it Cranium 17.
>
> And Cranium 17 had two holes in the forehead. Almost identical in shape and
> size. Both above the left eye. Both, on their own, likely fatal.
>
> Here's why that matters: an accidental fall tends to break one side of the
> skull, from one impact, at one angle. These wounds came from the same kind
> of object, striking twice, from two different angles. In forensic terms —
> that's not a fall. That's a person, swinging something, more than once.
>
> The remains are at least four hundred and thirty thousand years old. Before
> our species — Homo sapiens — even existed. This wasn't left by us. It was
> left by an earlier human relative entirely.
>
> Which would make it the oldest suspected murder ever found.
>
> I have to be straight with you here, because this channel doesn't do fake
> certainty: not everyone agrees. Some researchers argue the injuries could
> still have another explanation, and the pit itself — why dozens of bodies
> ended up there at all — is still debated. Falls. Predators. Something else
> entirely. Nobody's ruling it out completely.
>
> What almost everyone does agree on is this: whatever happened to Cranium
> 17, the instinct to end a rival with a rock is not new. It might be the
> oldest instinct we have.

### CASE 002 — THE OLDEST COLD CASE — 3:35–6:10
*(location card: the Ötztal Alps, Austria–Italy border — 1991)*

> September, 1991. Two German hikers, off the marked trail near a glacier,
> find a body sticking out of the melting ice. They assume it's a lost
> climber. Mountain rescue assumes the same.
>
> It takes days before anyone realizes what they actually found: a man,
> freeze-dried by the ice for over five thousand years. Skin, organs, even
> his last meal — all still intact.
>
> They name him Ötzi.
>
> For years, the leading theory was that he simply froze to death, alone, in
> a storm. Then, in two-thousand-one, a radiologist looked closer at an
> X-ray and found something everyone had missed: a stone arrowhead, lodged
> just under his left shoulder, buried in muscle.
>
> Someone had shot him in the back.
>
> The arrow tore through an artery near his collarbone. At that range, he
> would have bled out in minutes. Investigators — actual homicide
> detectives, the same ones who work modern cases — were brought in to
> reconstruct it. Their read: Ötzi was likely running, or fleeing, when he
> was hit from behind and below, meaning the shooter was some distance away,
> downhill.
>
> There's more. His hand carries a deep, healed defensive wound — from a
> knife fight days *before* he died. And traces of blood from at least two
> other people were found on his gear — one smear on an arrowhead, another
> on his knife.
>
> Put together, it reads like the final chapter of something much longer:
> a conflict, a fight he survived, and then, days later, an ambush he
> didn't.
>
> Ötzi is, formally, the oldest cold case on Earth. Full forensic file. Known
> cause of death. Known weapon.
>
> And after more than thirty years of investigation — zero suspects. The
> case is, and will always be, open.

>
> Until, in Mesopotamia, someone finally did.

### CASE 003 — THE FIRST LAW — 6:25–8:10
*(location card: Ur, Sumer, Mesopotamia — c. 2100 BCE)*

> Over two thousand years before Rome. Over three centuries before the more
> famous Code of Hammurabi. A Sumerian king named Ur-Nammu had a set of laws
> carved into clay tablets — and it survives today as the oldest complete
> legal code ever discovered.
>
> Here's what's genuinely striking about it: it isn't just "an eye for an
> eye." For most bodily harm, Ur-Nammu's code prescribes financial
> compensation — you break someone's bone, you pay a fixed price in silver.
> That's a radically modern idea for four thousand years ago: punishment
> scaled to the crime, not just raw revenge.
>
> But for the worst offenses — murder, robbery, and rape — there was no fine
> that covered it. Those were capital crimes. Full stop.
>
> Think about what this document actually represents. For the first time in
> the archaeological record, a society didn't just react to violence — it
> pre-defined it. It wrote down, in advance, exactly what would count as a
> crime, and exactly what would happen to you if you committed one.
>
> That is the moment "crime" stops being just something terrible that
> happens — and starts being something a society formally decides to punish.
> Every court, every police force, every true-crime case you've ever heard
> of is a distant descendant of this clay tablet.

---

### TRANSITION — 8:10–8:25

> Laws punish a crime after the body's already found. They do nothing for
> the killer who's patient enough to make it look like nothing happened at
> all.
>
> Which brings us to Rome — and the first killer we know of who did this
> again, and again, and again, for a living.

---

### CASE 004 — THE FIRST SERIAL KILLER — 8:25–10:40
*(location card: Rome, 1st century AD)*

> Her name was Locusta. Born in Gaul — roughly modern France — she grew up
> with an expert's knowledge of plants: which ones healed you, and which
> ones killed you slowly enough to look natural.
>
> In first-century Rome, that made her one of the most valuable, and most
> dangerous, people in the empire.
>
> She was first imprisoned for selling poison. But Empress Agrippina had a
> problem — her husband, the Emperor Claudius — and Agrippina had a plan for
> her son Nero to take the throne instead. Locusta was pulled out of prison
> to solve that problem.
>
> The job took two attempts. The first dose wasn't fatal. According to the
> Roman historian Tacitus, Locusta simply refined it — and delivered it
> again, this time hidden in Claudius's favorite dish of mushrooms.
>
> Claudius died. Nero took power. And Locusta wasn't punished — she was
> promoted. Given land, students, and the Emperor's protection, essentially
> running a state-sponsored poison workshop.
>
> Her next target, on Nero's order, was Britannicus — Claudius's own son,
> and Nero's rival for the throne. That poisoning is recorded as happening
> at dinner, in front of witnesses, disguised as a sudden seizure.
>
> This is why historians call Locusta one of the earliest documented serial
> killers in the record: multiple victims, a repeatable method, and — for
> years — an employer who protected her instead of a court that punished
> her. When Nero eventually died, his successor had her executed. But by
> then, the method she'd built had already outlived multiple emperors.

---

> For almost the entire span of human history after that, solving a murder
> meant one thing: find a witness, or force a confession. That was the
> entire toolkit.
>
> Then, in the year 1247, one man in China wrote down a method that had
> nothing to do with witnesses, or torture, or confessions at all.

### CASE 005 — THE FIRST FORENSIC SCIENTIST — 10:55–12:20
*(location card: China, Song Dynasty — 1247 AD)*

> His name was Song Ci — a coroner, and a judge. In 1247, he compiled
> everything he'd learned into a book called "The Washing Away of Wrongs."
> It's recognized today as the world's first textbook of forensic science.
>
> It's full of firsts: how to tell a drowning from a body dumped in water
> after death, how wound patterns reveal what weapon made them — and one
> case, from a village stabbing, that still gets taught today.
>
> A man is found dead, cut down with a bladed weapon. No witnesses. No
> confession. The local magistrate has every man in the village lay his
> sickle down in the dirt, in public, side by side.
>
> Nothing looked different. Every blade had already been wiped clean.
>
> Then the flies arrived. And they ignored every sickle in that row — except
> one. They swarmed a single blade, drawn to traces of blood and tissue too
> small for a human eye to see.
>
> The owner of that sickle confessed on the spot.
>
> That is the first recorded case of forensic entomology — insects, used as
> evidence — seven hundred years before crime labs existed. No confession
> extracted by force. No witness. Just a detail the killer didn't know he'd
> left behind, and a flaw in nature he never accounted for: he could clean a
> blade. He couldn't fool a fly.
